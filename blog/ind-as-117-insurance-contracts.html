1. What is IND AS 117 / IFRS 17?

IND AS 117 is the Indian Accounting Standard for insurance contracts, converged with IFRS 17 issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB). It replaces the old IND AS 104 (Insurance Contracts), which was widely criticised for allowing insurers to use legacy accounting policies rather than a consistent measurement framework.

The new standard introduces a single, principled measurement model for all insurance contracts — bringing much-needed transparency and comparability to how insurers report their financials. It is applicable to insurance companies following IND AS and is expected to be effective for Indian entities from FY 2026-27 onwards (subject to MCA notification).

The big idea: Under IND AS 117, profit from insurance can only be recognised as you actually provide insurance coverage — not upfront when you collect the premium. This is a fundamental shift from old practice.

2. Scope — Which Contracts Are Covered?

IND AS 117 applies to:

  • Insurance contracts issued by an entity (both direct insurance and reinsurance accepted)
  • Reinsurance contracts held by an entity (where the entity buys cover from a reinsurer)
  • Investment contracts with discretionary participation features (DPF) issued by entities that also issue insurance contracts

The standard does not apply to:

  • Warranties provided by manufacturers or retailers (covered by IND AS 115)
  • Employment benefit obligations (IND AS 19)
  • Financial guarantee contracts (IND AS 109, unless the issuer elects IND AS 117)
  • Fixed-fee service contracts such as roadside assistance contracts (IND AS 115)

Practical point: A contract transfers insurance risk when the insurer agrees to compensate the policyholder for a specified uncertain future event that adversely affects the policyholder. If there's no insurance risk, it's not an insurance contract under IND AS 117 — check your contract terms carefully.

3. Key Terms You Must Know

TermPlain English Meaning
Insurance ContractA contract where one party (insurer) accepts significant insurance risk from another party (policyholder) by agreeing to compensate if a specified uncertain event adversely affects the policyholder.
Coverage PeriodThe period during which the insurer provides insurance coverage — this is when revenue is recognised.
PolicyholderThe party that holds rights under an insurance contract (the insured person or entity).
Fulfillment Cash Flows (FCF)The expected present value of all future cash inflows (premiums) less outflows (claims, expenses) — plus a Risk Adjustment.
Contractual Service Margin (CSM)The unearned profit in a group of insurance contracts — released to P&L over the coverage period as the insurer provides services.
Risk Adjustment (RA)Compensation an insurer requires for bearing the uncertainty about the amount and timing of cash flows from non-financial risk.
Loss ComponentIf a group of contracts is onerous (expected to be loss-making), the loss is recognised immediately in P&L — there is no CSM for onerous contracts.
Discount RateReflects the time value of money and characteristics of the insurance contract cash flows. Typically based on risk-free rates adjusted for illiquidity.

4. Three Measurement Models

IND AS 117 provides three ways to measure insurance contracts. The right model depends on the nature and duration of the contract:

GMM / BBA

General Measurement Model (also called Building Block Approach). The default model. Used for long-term contracts like life insurance, annuities, and complex liability covers.

PAA

Premium Allocation Approach. Simplified model for short-duration contracts (coverage ≤ 12 months, or contracts where PAA produces similar results to GMM). Used for motor, health, fire, marine insurance.

VFA

Variable Fee Approach. Used for contracts where policyholders participate directly in returns from a defined pool of underlying items (e.g., unit-linked or participating life policies). Replaces the CSM with a variable fee.

Quick rule of thumb: Motor / health / fire insurance → PAA. Traditional life insurance (endowment, term, whole life) → GMM. Unit-linked / participating policies → VFA.

5. GMM in Depth: FCF + CSM

Under the General Measurement Model, the liability for a group of insurance contracts at any point in time has two components:

Liability for Remaining Coverage = Fulfillment Cash Flows (FCF) + Contractual Service Margin (CSM)
FCF = PV of Future Cash Outflows − PV of Future Cash Inflows + Risk Adjustment

Component 1: Fulfillment Cash Flows (FCF)

FCF captures the best-estimate present value of what the insurer expects to pay and receive over the remaining coverage period:

  • Expected claims — probability-weighted best estimate of future claim payments
  • Expected expenses — including claim handling costs and allocated overheads
  • Less: Expected future premiums — future instalments or renewals where the insurer has a substantive right
  • Plus: Risk Adjustment (RA) — explicit compensation for bearing non-financial risk uncertainty. The insurer estimates the amount it would require to be indifferent to an equivalent risk-free cash flow.

Component 2: Contractual Service Margin (CSM)

The CSM is the unearned profit locked inside the contract at inception. Think of it as a "profit reserve" that cannot be recognised immediately — it is released to P&L progressively as insurance services are provided.

  • At inception: CSM = Negative of FCF (i.e., if FCF is −₹50,000 meaning net inflow, CSM = +₹50,000)
  • If FCF is positive at inception (contract is onerous), there is no CSM — a loss is recognised immediately
  • CSM is released to Insurance Revenue over the coverage period based on "coverage units" provided in the period

Subsequent Measurement

Each period, the insurer updates FCF for changes in assumptions (mortality, morbidity, interest rates, expenses). Changes in FCF that relate to future services adjust the CSM (they don't hit P&L). Changes that relate to current or past services, or financial risk changes at locked-in rates, flow to P&L immediately.

6. How Revenue is Presented

This is where IND AS 117 differs most dramatically from old practice. Previously, insurers showed gross premium income as revenue. Under IND AS 117:

Insurance Revenue ≠ Premium Received. Insurance Revenue equals the amount of services provided in the period — which is the CSM released + expected claims for the period + risk adjustment released. The premium collected is not income until coverage is delivered.

The Income Statement under IND AS 117 looks like this:

Line ItemDescription
Insurance RevenueServices provided: expected claims + expenses + RA released + CSM released
Insurance Service ExpensesActual claims incurred + actual expenses + changes in loss component
Insurance Service ResultRevenue − Service Expenses (the underwriting profit)
Finance Income / ExpenseUnwinding of discount on FCF and CSM (interest effect)
Net Insurance Finance ResultFinance income less finance expense
Profit / LossInsurance Service Result + Finance Result + Investment Returns

7. Worked Example 1 — Motor Insurance (PAA)

Let's walk through a simple motor insurance policy using the Premium Allocation Approach.

Policy details: Annual motor insurance policy · Premium ₹12,000 collected on 1 October 2024 · Policy period: 1 Oct 2024 to 30 Sep 2025 · Financial year end: 31 March 2025 · Expected claims ratio: 65% · Acquisition costs: ₹900 (paid upfront)

Under PAA, the liability for remaining coverage is essentially the unearned premium — very similar to the old "Unexpired Risk Reserve" concept but computed more rigorously.

On day one, the insurer collects ₹12,000 and recognises a liability for the full unearned premium. Acquisition costs of ₹900 are recognised as an asset (Deferred Acquisition Costs — DAC) and amortised over the coverage period.

8. Worked Example 2 — Life Insurance (GMM)

Now let's look at a long-term life insurance policy under the General Measurement Model.

Policy details: 5-year term life insurance · Annual premium ₹20,000 (paid each April 1) · Sum Assured ₹20,00,000 on death · Inception: 1 April 2024 · Discount rate: 7% p.a. · Best-estimate annual claim cost (mortality): ₹4,000 · Annual expenses: ₹1,500 · Risk Adjustment: ₹2,500 at inception

Step 1: Calculate FCF at Inception (1 April 2024)

FCF = PV of expected future outflows (claims + expenses) − PV of future premiums + Risk Adjustment

FCF is negative ₹46,611 — meaning the contract has a net expected inflow (profitable). Therefore CSM = +₹46,611.

Initial CSM = −FCF = ₹46,611. This is the unearned profit that will be released over 5 years of coverage. The insurer cannot book this profit upfront.

Step 2: Journal Entry at Inception (1 April 2024)

At inception, the insurer receives the first year's premium of ₹20,000 and recognises the insurance contract liability. The liability at inception = FCF + CSM = (−₹46,611) + ₹46,611 = ₹0. This makes intuitive sense — on day one, the insurer has collected one premium and the two components exactly offset.

10. Transition Approaches

IND AS 117 provides three transition approaches for the opening balance at the date of initial application. All three produce the same liability at transition — but the way historical data is used differs:

  • Full Retrospective Approach — Apply IND AS 117 as if it had always existed. Most accurate but data-intensive. Requires historical assumption data going back to contract inception.
  • Modified Retrospective Approach — Apply IND AS 117 retrospectively but with certain practical modifications where full retrospective data is unavailable. Strikes a balance between accuracy and practicality.
  • Fair Value Approach — Determine the CSM at transition as the difference between the IND AS 117 fair value of the group and the FCF at that date. Least data-intensive; acceptable when historical data is limited.

Most Indian insurers are expected to use the Modified Retrospective or Fair Value approach given the practical challenges of sourcing historical assumption sets for contracts issued decades ago (e.g., old endowment policies).

11. Key Disclosures

IND AS 117 requires extensive disclosures to help users understand how insurance contracts affect the financial statements. The main requirements (Para 93–132) include:

  • Reconciliation of opening and closing balances of the insurance contract assets / liabilities — showing movements due to new contracts, CSM release, changes in estimates, experience variances, and finance effects
  • Insurance Revenue and expenses breakdown — how revenue is split between claims, expenses, RA release, and CSM release
  • Maturity analysis — undiscounted expected cash flows for remaining coverage by time bucket
  • Judgements and assumptions — significant assumptions used in estimating FCF; sensitivity analysis for key variables (mortality, morbidity, lapse rates, discount rates)
  • Transition disclosures — which approach was applied and why; quantitative impact at transition

12. Frequently Asked Questions

Is IND AS 117 effective in India right now?

As of June 2026, IND AS 117 has not yet been notified by MCA for mandatory application in India. It is expected to be effective for Indian insurance companies from FY 2026-27, with a comparative period of FY 2025-26. Insurance entities should begin impact assessments and systems implementation now.

Does IND AS 117 apply to general insurance companies too?

Yes. IND AS 117 applies to all entities that issue insurance contracts — both life and non-life (general) insurance companies, as well as reinsurers. Non-life insurers with mostly short-duration contracts will typically use the PAA model, which is simpler and requires less data than GMM.

What happens to Day 1 profit under IND AS 117?

Under IND AS 117, any profit expected from a profitable group of insurance contracts at inception is deferred into the CSM and cannot be recognised immediately. It is released progressively as the insurer provides coverage services. This prevents insurers from front-loading profits and gives users a more faithful picture of earnings quality.

How is the CSM different from a deferred revenue?

The CSM is conceptually similar to deferred revenue but goes further — it specifically represents the unearned profit margin, not gross unearned premiums. The FCF component handles expected claims and expenses separately, so the CSM is a pure profit deferral. Under old IND AS 104, the closest equivalent was the Unexpired Risk Reserve, but it was not required to reflect a specific profit margin.

Can a group of insurance contracts have a negative CSM?

No. If FCF at inception shows that a group of contracts is onerous (expected loss), the CSM is set to zero and the expected loss is recognised immediately in P&L via a "loss component." There is no negative CSM. This is a significant difference from the old Liability Adequacy Test, which was only a catch-up mechanism.

How does IND AS 117 affect key ratios for insurers?

Several financial metrics change significantly. The combined ratio calculation changes because insurance revenue no longer equals gross premiums. Return on equity patterns shift because profits emerge over the coverage period rather than upfront. Investors and analysts will need to recalibrate their models. Insurers should proactively communicate the financial impact to analysts during the transition period.
